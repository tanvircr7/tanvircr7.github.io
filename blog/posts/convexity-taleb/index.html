A summary of research strategies that leverage convexity, optionality, and antifragility to exploit randomness for scientific and technological breakthroughs.
Published

September 28, 2025

Introduction: Missing the Asymmetry

Did you just get lucky or was it your aim?
Linear, teleological success stories fit physics.
In opaque, nonlinear fields (medicine, engineering), chance drives more breakthroughs.
Still, we build research programs around stories and direction—despite knowing better.

Key Point:
Chance alone can’t yield steady progress—unless the payoff function is asymmetric. Nonlinear payoffs let us systematically benefit from randomness.

  • Welcome uncertainty in the long run. It can hurt you less than it helps you.

Opaque Systems and Optionality

Trial and error + convexity = real progress
In experiments (cooking, science), keep what works, discard what doesn’t.
We have the option (not the obligation) to retain only favorable results.
This “optionality”—the ability to choose after the fact—lies behind convex research payoffs.

  • Optionality liberates us from inflexible roadmaps, predictions, and stories.
  • Some research results have abnormally large long-term impact (penicillin, black swans, GPT-5 scale events).

Analogy: Research vs. Financial Options

  • Research is like running many small, cheap experiments (like owning lots of “call options”).
  • Failures cost little; one big win changes everything.
  • In finance, option buyers pay a premium and the game is zero-sum.
  • In research, no one sells you upside or limits your payoff:
    • One discovery grows the overall pie of knowledge, not just shifting value between players.

Why Uncertainty Is Valuable

  • Which projects should be funded?
    • Mathematical Principle: In convex systems, both convexity (asymmetry of payoff) and volatility (randomness) work in your favor.
  • Gains > Harms if the payoff is asymmetric.

Convexity Bias (Jensen’s Inequality)

  • Suppose a coin:
    • Linear: win $1, lose $1. EV = $0.
    • Convex: win $10, lose $1. EV = +$4.5.
    • The more convex the scenario, the higher the built-in advantage.
  • Jensen’s inequality: Convex functions benefit from randomness.

Bottom Line:
- The more convex the payoff, and the more uncertain the environment, the greater the net expected gain.

Antifragility: Benefiting From Disorder

  • Fragile things dislike volatility (coffee cups).
  • Antifragile things love volatility (evolution, discovery).
  • Convexity bias (not mere luck) can be defined, measured, and used to guide research funding.

Seven Rules of Antifragile (Convex) Research

  1. Convexity > Knowledge
    • Improving the payoff shape (limiting downside, stretching up side) matters more than understanding the target deeply. Lower costs per attempt to maximize upside from uncertainty.
  2. 1/N Strategy for Convex Bets
    • Spread the budget widely: make N as large as possible, allocating 1/N to each experiment/project.
    • “Fat tails” mean rare events dominate long-term results—don’t put all your eggs in one basket, and don’t try to pick the winner in advance.
  3. Serial Optionality
    • Plans must allow frequent adjustments (“highways with many exits”).
    • Short, flexible investments vastly outperform rigid, long-term bets.
  4. Nonnarrative Research
    • Favor action and opportunistic agents over story-driven plans.
    • Track records come from those who keep switching until something works, not from those who guess the ending in advance.
  5. Practice → Theory
    • Major breakthroughs often follow from tinkering (convex practice) rather than top-down theory.
  6. Premium for Simplicity
    • The simplest technologies are easily missed; avoid needless complexity.
    • Simple, modular setups are more robust in uncertain, opaque environments.
  7. Catalog Negative Results (“Via Negativa”)
    • Knowing what doesn’t work is powerful. Fund, capture, and spread negative results to reduce wasted effort.

Is All This Just Lottery Thinking?

Critics say: “This sounds like buying lottery tickets.”
But key differences make research optionality superior:

  • You control your “ticket price” and can lower trial costs.
  • Upside in research can be unlimited; lotteries have capped payoffs.
  • You keep only winnings and cut losses via optionality.
  • Learning and adaptation can increase your odds over time (lotteries are static).
  • Diversification (many independent tries) raises expected value—unlike buying more lottery tickets.
  • Research creates positive-sum value (society wins); lotteries are zero-sum with a house edge.
  • Hits spawn new opportunities; lottery wins don’t.

Quick Math: - 100 research trials × $1 = $100 spent; if one scores $200 and rest fail, EV = +$101. - 100 lottery tickets × $1 = $100; average return $0.60 per ticket: EV = −$40.

To avoid “lottery-like” waste: - Keep trials cheap - Ensure upside is not capped - Avoid correlated bets - Learn as you go and cut losses fast

Appendix: Visual Intuitions

  1. Fat vs. thin tails: Rarer outcomes matter much more in fat-tailed systems.
  2. Right tail (lognormal): Small set of rare wins dominate value.
  3. Left tail (negative events): Most events harmless, but must cap rare disasters.
  4. Operations: Tails, not averages, dictate most real-life quality (e.g., user pain in computing).

Rule of thumb: Cap the left tail (risks), keep the right tail (potential).
